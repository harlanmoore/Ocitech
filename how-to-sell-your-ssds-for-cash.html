How to sell your SSDs for the most cash.

What used and pulled SSDs are actually worth, the details that move the price up or down, and how to turn a pile of drives — or bare flash boards — into the highest possible payment. From a buyer that's been doing this since 2001.

First: what your SSDs are actually worth

Used SSDs are not priced like used laptops. A drive's value tracks the NAND flash and controller inside it far more than the sticker on the case, and the used market moves with the memory cycle — sometimes sharply, week to week. Two drives that look identical can be worth very different amounts depending on interface, capacity, endurance rating, and how much life is left on the flash. Before you accept the first offer or dump a lot on a marketplace, it's worth understanding the handful of things a serious buyer actually pays for. Get those right and the same pile of drives can be worth substantially more.

Enterprise vs. consumer — the biggest single lever

The first thing that sets your price is whether you're holding enterprise/data-center drives or consumer drives, because they sell into completely different markets.

Enterprise & data-center SSDs

U.2, U.3, and enterprise M.2 and SAS drives from lines like Intel/Solidigm (DC P-series, D-series), Samsung (PM-series), Micron (7000/9000-series), and Kioxia (CD/CM-series) hold their value best. They ship in volume out of decommissioned servers, they carry high endurance ratings, and there's steady downstream demand for them — so this is where the real money is, especially in bulk.

Consumer & mixed drives

SATA and consumer NVMe drives (the M.2 sticks out of desktops and laptops) are worth less per unit, but they're absolutely still worth selling in quantity. Don't throw them in with the enterprise lot at one blended price — separating them usually nets you more.

Quick tell: if the drive came out of a rack server, has a U.2/U.3 connector or a 12 Gb SAS interface, and the label shows a high TBW or DWPD rating, it's enterprise — price it as such. Don't let a buyer blend it into a consumer-grade offer.

The four details that set the price

Once the enterprise/consumer split is clear, four attributes do most of the work in a quote. Read them off the label before you ask for a number.

CapacityLarger drives (3.84TB, 7.68TB, 15.36TB) carry a premium per GB, not just more total value
Interface / form factorNVMe (U.2, U.3, M.2) generally over SATA; SAS priced on demand — list what you have
Endurance & healthTBW/DWPD rating and remaining life (SMART wear indicator, power-on hours) — low wear is worth more
Model / part numberThe single most useful field — it encodes the maker, generation, capacity, and grade

If you can pull a SMART report — even a screenshot of the wear-leveling / percentage-used and power-on hours — it lets a buyer firm up a number instead of quoting conservatively against the unknown. Low-hour, low-wear drives are worth more, and being able to prove it is worth real money on a large lot.

Tested, untested, pulls — and bare boards

You don't need to test anything to sell it, but knowing which bucket you're in helps you get the right number.

  • Tested / working — the top of the range. If you've already verified drives and have SMART data, say so.
  • Pulls / untested — drives pulled from working systems but not individually tested. Completely normal to sell; priced with a modest discount for the unknown.
  • Failed / for recovery — even dead drives have value: the NAND and controller can be recovered.
  • Bare flash boards & cut boards — populated PCBs and cut boards are priced at the chip level, on the NAND part numbers or FBGA marking codes they carry. If that's what you have, that's a market of its own.
Selling loose NAND, cut boards, or populated PCBs rather than whole drives? That's priced on the chips themselves — see sell NAND flash & memory chips, where we list hundreds of the exact part numbers and FBGA codes we're buying now.

Data sanitization — do it right, and it helps your price

Any drive leaving your hands should have its data dealt with, and doing it properly is both the responsible move and a selling point. A drive that's been securely erased (ATA Secure Erase, NVMe Format with a cryptographic erase, or the manufacturer's sanitize command) stays fully functional and resells at full value — unlike a physically shredded drive, which is worth only scrap. If your data policy allows secure erasure rather than destruction, you keep the drive's resale value and still meet your compliance requirement. A reputable buyer will also provide documentation — a certificate of data sanitization or destruction — for your records. If you can't erase them yourself, that's fine; just flag it, and make sure your buyer sanitizes and certifies.

Selling in bulk: packing and logistics

For a lot of any size, how you present and ship it affects both the offer and how fast you get paid. A rough count by category — "about 200 mixed enterprise U.2, mostly 3.84TB Samsung and Intel, pulled from decommissioned servers" — is enough to get a real quote started; you don't need a perfectly sorted spreadsheet. When it's time to ship, keep drives from rattling loose: anti-static packaging for enterprise NVMe, trays or original packaging where you have them, and sturdy boxes. On larger lots a buyer will usually cover or arrange freight. The cleaner and better-described the lot, the higher and firmer the number.

Why a broker beats flipping them yourself

For one or two drives, a marketplace like eBay is fine. For a lot — dozens or hundreds of drives, or bare boards — it rarely is. Listing individually eats hours, you pay selling fees and shipping on every unit, you carry the fraud and return risk, and enterprise U.2/SAS drives simply don't have a deep consumer audience. A broker gives you one quote for the whole lot, one shipment, one payment, and takes on the testing, grading, and downstream selling. You trade a slightly lower per-unit headline for far less work, far less risk, and — on volume — often more money in hand after fees. That's the whole point of selling to a buyer who moves this stuff every week.

The short version — get the most cash

  • Separate enterprise/data-center drives from consumer drives; don't sell them at one blended price.
  • List interface, capacity, and part numbers — and pull SMART/wear data if you can.
  • Securely erase (don't shred) where your policy allows, and get a sanitization certificate.
  • Sell the whole lot to a volume buyer instead of flipping units one at a time.
  • Selling bare NAND or cut boards? Price them at the chip level on their part numbers.

Questions

Selling your SSDs — common questions.

How do I get the most money for used SSDs?

Separate enterprise/data-center drives from consumer drives, list the interface, capacity, and part numbers, and include SMART/wear data if you can pull it. Securely erase rather than shred where your policy allows, so the drives keep full resale value, and sell the whole lot to a volume buyer instead of listing units one at a time. Those steps together usually beat a quick marketplace dump by a wide margin.

Do you buy untested or failed SSDs?

Yes. Working drives price highest, but pulls, untested lots, and even failed drives all have value — the NAND flash and controller can be recovered. Just tell us roughly what condition they're in; you don't need to test anything first.

What about the data on the drives?

Any drive should be sanitized before it changes hands. A secure erase (ATA Secure Erase, NVMe cryptographic erase, or the manufacturer's sanitize command) wipes the data while keeping the drive fully functional and worth full value — far better than shredding, which leaves only scrap. If you can't erase them yourself, we sanitize on our end and can provide a certificate of data sanitization for your records.

Can I sell bare flash boards or cut boards instead of whole drives?

Yes — that's a market of its own. Populated PCBs, pulled boards, and cut boards are priced at the chip level, on the NAND part numbers or Micron FBGA marking codes they carry. Send the part numbers or a photo of the chip markings and we'll price it.

Is it worth selling SSDs in bulk rather than on eBay?

For a lot, almost always. Listing drives individually costs you hours, selling fees, and shipping on every unit, plus the return and fraud risk — and enterprise U.2/SAS drives have little consumer audience. A broker gives you one quote, one shipment, and one payment for the whole lot, and often more in hand after fees.
